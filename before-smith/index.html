THE MYSTERY

Every theory of profit was wearing a mask

Between 1500 and Adam Smith, economic thinkers kept changing their minds about what gives things value and where profit comes from. Hunt and Lautzenheiser's answer is that the ideas followed the power. Whoever held economic power, first merchants and then producer-capitalists, shaped the theory of the day. That wasn't foolishness. Each theory described the economy its authors could see.

1

How will we make it?

Buy low, sell high, or put workers to work? The masters become employers.

How will we share it?

Poor Laws, wage limits, and a French doctor's map of where the surplus flows.

CLUE 1

What will we make?

The merchant era said to make whatever sells, and export as much of it as possible. Value is just market price, driven by usefulness and scarcity. Later, Mandeville claimed that greed and vanity are what keep the whole hive making things.

Barbon's price machine

Nicholas Barbon said a thing is worth whatever it sells for. Usefulness drives the wanting, and plenty or scarcity sets the price. Pick a good and change how much is on the market.

The export machine

Mercantilists wanted exports to exceed imports. Money would flow in, prices would rise, and above all people would have work. Full employment was the thread running through their policies.

Mandeville's beehive

In The Fable of the Bees (1714), a grumbling hive of vice prospers. When it turns honest, trade collapses. The old sins turn out to be what keep the economy busy. Switch the vices on and off.

CLUE 2

How will we make it?

At first, workers still owned their tools and the lords took the surplus. Merchants profited by moving goods, not by making them. Then competition squeezed trading profits, guild masters turned into employers, and thinkers began to say labor is what creates value.

Buy low, sell high

A merchant buys pepper where it's cheap and sells it in London where it's dear. Then other merchants smell the profit. Add rivals, or get a royal charter to shut them out.

Mechanism from pp. 24–25 and 28; numbers illustrative.

Trading goods = trading labor

Once work was divided into specialties, Mandeville saw that when a weaver's cloth is swapped for a baker's bread, what's really being exchanged is labor for labor. Raw nature only becomes a commodity after labor has worked on it. Writers like Cary and Hay went further: profit is the surplus left after workers have what they need to live. What none of them could yet explain was how labor could be both the measure of price and the source of profit. That puzzle was left for Adam Smith.

pp. 33–35
CLUE 3

How will we share it?

When the Church lost its power, the Tudor state took over the care of the poor and treated poverty as a failure of the system, not the person. But the same state capped wages for the employers' benefit. Across the Channel, Quesnay drew the first map of where the surplus goes.

The maximum wage

The Statute of Artificers (1563) set the highest wages a worker could be paid. The justices who set them were usually employers. Prices rose faster than the caps.

p. 27; growth rates illustrative.

A paternal state

The Poor Laws assumed poverty "was a function of the economic system," so those who benefited from the system should support its victims. Parishes had to levy a compulsory poor rate (1572), and the poor gained a formal right to relief (1601).

But paternalism never meant raising the poor up. Like the medieval lords, Tudor monarchs believed the lower classes should be kept in their place.

Two centuries later, Malthus would attack these same Poor Laws. See Case 003 →

pp. 26–28

Quesnay's Tableau économique · follow the money

The Physiocrats' model of France: farmers (the "productive" class) grow a surplus, pay it to the landlords (the "idle" class) as rent, and landlords spend it on food and on goods from the artisans (the "sterile" class). As long as money keeps circulating, next year starts where this one did. But what if the landlords hoard part of their rent?

Flows simplified from pp. 36–37 (in milliards of livres); the hoarding path is illustrative. The authors note this anticipates Malthus's gluts and Keynes.
THE VERDICT

Let's see who these ghosts really are

Each era's theory haunted economics like a ghost. Pull off the mask and you'll find the class whose interests it served.

Echoes in later cases

🐝 Mandeville's private vices, public benefits → Smith's invisible hand.

💸 Quesnay's hoarding jams → Malthus's gluts (and later Keynes).

🧹 The Physiocrats' unproductive labor → Malthus's "personal services."

🏚️ The 1601 Poor Law → the law Malthus attacked in 1798.

CASE CLOSED?

Not quite

These thinkers sensed that labor was behind profit, but couldn't explain how. In 1776 Adam Smith picked up the trail.
