Chapter 4 · Thomas Robert Malthus

Every economy answers three questions

What gets made, how it gets made, and who gets what. Malthus answered all three during Britain's industrial revolution, and in Hunt and Lautzenheiser's reading, his answer to the third question, who gets what, shaped his answers to the other two.

1
What to make?

Food or factory goods? Goods to use now or machines for later? Can a society make too much?
2
How to make it?

Factories, land of uneven fertility, and Malthus's view of production as a trade between three owners.
3
How to share it?

Workers vs. owners, then landlords vs. capitalists. Rents, the Corn Laws, and gluts.

The world Malthus wrote in

Two class conflicts ran through his lifetime. Workers against owners: industrialization was built on falling or flat living standards, child labor, slums, and laws banning unions. Landlords against capitalists: the old landed class still ran Parliament and fought the rising industrialists over whether Britain would stay agricultural or become the workshop of the world.

Two Malthuses

■ 1790s–1800s · Essay on Population

Opponent: workers and radical reformers. Claim: poverty is a law of nature, and helping the poor makes it worse.

■ 1814 onward · Principles

Opponent: industrial capitalists. Claim: the landlords' interest is the nation's interest, and their spending prevents depressions.

Question 1

What will we make?

For Malthus, food limits everything. Population can grow geometrically and food only arithmetically, so the first thing any society must produce is subsistence, and it will never produce enough of it for everyone.

Malthus's own defaults: doubling every 25 years, food rising by a constant amount. He put the ratio after two centuries at 256 to 9. (p. 74–75)

What closes the gap? The "checks"

Something must keep population down to the food supply. Malthus sorted these checks two ways. Switch between the schemes.

Farm or factory?

The Corn Laws fight was really about what Britain would make. Landlords wanted a self-sufficient farming nation. Industrialists wanted Britain to specialize in manufactures and import cheap grain.

Malthus sided with farming. More food supports more people, and those people must eat, so agriculture creates its own demand. He argued that more manufactures don't.

pp. 70–71, 82

Goods now or machines later?

Industrialization meant putting resources into factories and machinery, which left less for everyday consumption. In the authors' account, that sacrifice fell on the class with the least power: the workers, whose real wages fell as grain prices soared.

pp. 65–66

What counts as wealth?

Smith
The produce of labor.
Malthus
Useful material objects people own, even things no labor went into.

He also renamed "unproductive labor" as personal services (servants, artists, clergy). This mattered later: his cure for depressions depends on those services.

p. 80

Can a society make too much?

Most classical economists said no, because whatever is saved gets spent on new capital. Malthus said yes: in a general glut, goods pile up unsold because people with income aren't spending it. His answer to "what to make" therefore included a surprising item: enough luxury consumption by people who don't produce. Try the glut machine under Question 3.

Question 2

How will we make it?

The factory system answered this question in practice. Malthus answered it in theory by treating production as a trade between three kinds of owner. Hunt and Lautzenheiser argue that this viewpoint hides where the surplus comes from.

The factory answer

Machines set the pace, the clock set the day, and work was split into routine tasks that women and children could do for lower wages. Workers lost control of their tools and their time and became appendages of the machine. The Luddites smashed machines until 1813, when many were hanged or deported.

pp. 66–67

The labor lag

Malthus noted that new capital can appear in a year, but a new adult worker takes 16–18 years. So a sudden investment boom finds too few hands: some capital sits idle and wages rise.

The authors' catch: by his own population theory, population should adjust to any steady rate of investment. The lag only makes sense if you quietly set the population theory aside.

pp. 85–86

As population grows, poorer land comes under the plow. The price of grain must rise to cover costs on the worst land farmed, so the better land earns a surplus: rent. Malthus called this surplus a gift of God's bounty in the soil and treated it as the landlord's contribution. Rising rents, he argued, are the best sign of national prosperity. Rent theory is really about how food is grown, but it leads straight to Question 3: who keeps the surplus?

Question 3

How will we share it?

This is the question the chapter is really about. The price of everything splits into wages, profits, and rent, and those are also the incomes of the three classes. Whatever one class gains, another loses.

Round 1 · Owners vs. workers

Malthus's population theory made redistribution pointless in his eyes. Raise the incomes of the poor and they will have more children until everyone is back at bare subsistence. So he opposed the poor laws, charitable wage top-ups, and in one notorious passage even medical cures.

"…in the great lottery of life, have drawn a blank."
Malthus, first Essay (1798), on the poor

Only "moral restraint," meaning late marriage without vice, could lift someone out of poverty. The authors conclude that for Malthus, the difference between rich and poor came down to character.

pp. 73–78

The alternatives he fought

Condorcet: a public fund for the old, widows, and orphans, and credit steered away from big capitalists toward ordinary workers.

Godwin: private property causes crime and misery, and the rich write the laws, so government will never fix it. Reason would lead people to abolish property, classes, and the state.

Malthus replied that any such society would soon fall back into a class of proprietors and a class of laborers, by natural law.

pp. 72–74

Round 2 · The glut machine

Output is worth 100, and that 100 is also everyone's income. Workers and landlords spend all of theirs. Capitalists save most of their profit and can only invest part of it profitably. Anything saved but not invested means goods no one buys.

The double standard

Try both buttons. Moving 10 points from profits to rent and moving 10 points to wages close the glut by exactly the same amount and cut profits by exactly the same amount. Malthus endorsed the first (the Corn Laws, landlords' servants, "personal services") and rejected the second on the grounds that higher wages lower profits and kill the motive to invest. Higher rents do that too. The authors read this as class loyalty overriding logic.

pp. 86–88

Debate & verdict

Malthus vs. Ricardo

After 1819, Malthus and David Ricardo, the capitalists' leading spokesman, argued over gluts. Step through the exchange.

Malthus
Capitalists save far faster than capital can profitably grow. Spending falls short and goods go unsold: a general glut.
Ricardo
Nonsense. Whatever is saved is spent on new capital. Supply creates its own market.
Malthus
Then we need a class that consumes more than it produces. The landlords stand preeminent: they and their servants spend without adding to supply. Keep rents high.
Ricardo
Giving goods to people who return nothing doesn't raise my profits. Hand soldiers the clothier's cloth and it stimulates him "in the same way as a fire would."
Malthus
But raising wages won't do. Workers' consumption raises costs, lowers profits, and destroys the motive to accumulate.
Hunt & Lautzenheiser
Both are half right. Each capitalist wants their own wages and rents low, but all capitalists together need those incomes to buy their goods. Malthus saw the demand problem; Ricardo saw the cost problem. Neither could solve the contradiction, which returns with Keynes (ch. 15).

Paraphrased from pp. 84–89; the quoted phrase is Ricardo's own.

The three questions, answered

QuestionMalthus's answerThe authors' critique
WhatFood first; farming over manufacturing; enough unproductive luxury consumption to prevent gluts.His definition of wealth leaves out labor, and his glut remedy quietly contradicts his population theory.
HowThree owners (labor, capital, land) each supply a necessary input and earn a matching reward.That treats owning as producing. Tools are stored-up past labor, and the exchange view hides where the surplus comes from.
SharePoverty is natural and permanent; landlords' high rents serve everyone.His conclusions follow his class loyalties, not his logic. Wages vs. rent is the tell.

Lasting legacy

The population theory inspired Darwin and lives on in theories of less-developed countries. The glut theory, his most important contribution in the authors' view, anticipates Keynes on insufficient demand.
