Three thinkers swapped labor for utility (pleasure) as the source of value. With that one change, class conflict vanished and economics became a story of perfect harmony. Hunt and Lautzenheiser ask whether it's a dream.

THE BIG SWITCH

From labor to pleasure

Smith and Ricardo said labor creates value, and that put workers and owners at odds. Bentham, Say, and Senior said value comes from utility, the pleasure a thing gives. With that, working and owning look like the same kind of contribution, everyone is paid what they deserve, and the market is good for all. The authors call this "rationalistic subjectivism," and argue it laid the foundation for most economics since.

QUESTION 1

What will we make?

Whatever gives people pleasure. Value now comes from utility, not labor. And since wants seem endless, whatever gets made will always find a buyer… or will it?

Jeremy Bentham1748–1832
"All value is founded on utility."

Bentham took on Smith's water-and-diamonds puzzle (why does useful water cost nothing while useless diamonds cost a fortune?). His answer comes close to what later economists called marginal utility: what matters is the value of the next glass, and when you already have plenty, the next one is worth almost nothing.

If the whole quantity required is available, the surplus has no kind of value.Jeremy Bentham, on water
But later… Bentham also noticed that savings can sit unspent "in a chest," so the economy may make less than it could. Then, he said, the government should add money.
pp. 131–33
Jean-Baptiste Say1767–1832
"Produce opens a vent for produce."

Say's law: nobody makes things except to trade them for other things, so supply always creates an equal demand. There can be too much of one good, but only because there's too little of another, and capital moving between industries fixes that. A general glut is impossible.

Exchangeable value, or price, is an index of the recognized utility of a thing.Jean-Baptiste Say, A Treatise on Political Economy
The authors' catch: recurring depressions kept happening. Malthus, the later Bentham, Marx, and Keynes all rejected Say's law.
pp. 134–38; quantities illustrative.
Nassau Senior1790–1864
Everyone always wants more.

Senior's first proposition: every person wants more wealth for as little sacrifice as possible, and "no person feels his whole wants to be adequately supplied." If wants are endless, nothing made can ever go unsold, so a glut is impossible. He went further than Say and hardly admitted slumps had happened at all.

Pleasure from the next thing you buy
The authors' catch: Senior's own premise of insatiable desire "proved" depressions couldn't exist, so he simply looked past the ones that did.
pp. 144–46
QUESTION 2

How will we make it?

With calculating minds and painful sacrifices. Every worker endures the pain of labor; every owner endures the pain of saving. So labor, capital, and land become equal partners in making utility.

Jeremy Benthamthe pleasure calculator
"Nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure."

Bentham believed pleasure could be measured like physics, on seven dimensions. Every decision is a sum: add the pleasures, subtract the pains, and do what comes out ahead. And work is pure pain. "Love of labour is a contradiction in terms."

The authors' catch: a capitalist's bookkeeping became the model for all human choice. Habit, kindness, faith, and feeling dropped out of the picture.
pp. 129–31; scoring is our playful take on his seven circumstances.
Jean-Baptiste Saythe three partners
"To the labour of man alone he ascribes the power of producing values. This is an error."

Say "corrected" Smith: labor, capital, and land are three equal productive agencies that together produce utility. Owning is as productive as working. Make a pink gown and see who made it, by Say's account and by Ricardo's.

The authors' catch: one historian compares Say to someone tidying a pair of trousers by turning them upside down and emptying out all the valuables. Safer that way: Say "purged" Smith of his dangerous ideas.
pp. 134–37
Nassau Seniorthe abstinence argument
Profit is the reward for abstinence.

Senior's most lasting idea. Workers sacrifice by laboring; capitalists sacrifice by abstaining from spending their capital on enjoyment. Both endure pain, so both deserve their pay. Build a capitalist and test how much sacrifice is really there.

Real sacrifice behind the profit
To abstain from the enjoyment which is in our power, or to seek distant rather than immediate results, are among the most painful exertions of the human will.Nassau Senior, An Outline of the Science of Political Economy
The authors' catch: most capital is inherited, and capitalists enjoy making profits. Senior himself admitted they "go on producing and amassing and leave the task of expending to their heirs."
pp. 146–50
QUESTION 3

How will we share it?

Bentham's math pointed toward sharing more equally. Say and Senior made every income look deserved, made the classes disappear, and sent the poor to the workhouse.

Jeremy Benthamthe happiness machine
Money brings less pleasure the more you have.

A rich man with 50,000 times a laborer's income isn't 50,000 times happier. Bentham thought even "twice" was generous. So moving money from rich to poor raises society's total happiness. He wanted inequality cut to the level of the "Anglo-American United States", which would have meant radical reform in England.

The authors' catch: reform needs an impartial government. But by Bentham's own theory, legislators are selfish too. Who pays them better, the rich or the poor?
pp. 133–34; happiness uses a simple diminishing curve.
Jean-Baptiste Saypaid by contribution
Each partner is paid for the utility it creates.

In Say's account each productive agency is paid in proportion to "the importance of its co-operation." Whatever the split turns out to be, it's what each side contributed, so it's always just. The rich and poor "are not opposed to each other, and that all rivalships are mere folly."

The authors' catch: there's no way to measure each agency's "importance" apart from the pay itself, so the theory justifies whatever split already exists. Say even said it makes "no difference whatever" whether property was gained by violence or fraud.
pp. 135–37
Nassau Seniorthe vanishing classes
Wages, profit, and rent blur together.

Senior said any earnings from an advantage others can't copy (a rare talent, an inherited fortune) are really rent. And a capitalist who manages must labor, so part of profit is really wages. Sort these incomes the way Senior would.
