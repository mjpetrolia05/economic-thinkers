CASE NO. 002 · 1723 – 1790 · SCOTLAND

How a society makes its living shapes everything else

For Smith, the way people produce and distribute the necessities of life decides their institutions: who owns what, who governs, who obeys. He saw class conflict clearly. Yet he also believed an invisible hand steers selfish, conflicting actions toward a harmony that nobody intended. Hunt and Lautzenheiser call this the most important incongruity, if not contradiction, in his writing.

I

What will we make?

Game, cattle, grain, then manufactures. And why a trick of nature makes people want baubles.
II

How will we make it?

Labor creates all wealth, tools are stored-up labor, and producers with no property won't work hard.
III

How will we share it?

Rent, wages, profit, and a wage bargain between unequal sides.

No society is bound to climb. It advances only when geography, economy, and culture allow, and the economic base shapes institutions without rigidly determining them. (p. 44)
QUESTION I

What will we make?

The answer changes with each stage: game in the forest, cattle on the steppe, grain on the manor, manufactures in the town. What pushes a society from one to the next is a desire that, in Smith's view, rests on an illusion.

THE LORD'S SURPLUS · rustic hospitality or baubles?

A great proprietor's land yields a surplus that can feed 100 people. Without towns or foreign trade there is nothing to buy, so he can only use it by keeping retainers, and people fed entirely by his bounty must obey him.

NATURE'S DECEPTION
It is this deception which rouses and keeps in continual motion the industry of mankind.
Adam Smith, Theory of Moral Sentiments

The illusion that builds cities

Nature fools people into thinking wealth brings happiness. Smith thought this was false, but he admired what it does: it drives people to farm, build, found cities, and invent. What gets made is steered by a desire nobody would defend on reflection.

p. 46

An unintended revolution

Lords traded their surplus for manufactures out of what Smith called "the most childish vanity." Merchants and artisans sold to them just to turn a penny. Neither side foresaw that together they were dissolving feudal power and creating commercial society.

p. 47
QUESTION II

How will we make it?

Smith was clear that labor makes wealth. Tools raise productivity, but tools are themselves the product of earlier labor. And people work hard only when they may own what they produce.

THE SOURCE OF WEALTH
It was not by gold or by silver, but by labour, that all the wealth of the world was originally purchased.
Adam Smith, Wealth of Nations
THE ONLY WAY TO GROW
…by increasing either the number of productive labourers, or the productive powers of those labourers.
Adam Smith, Wealth of Nations, on raising the annual produce

NO PROPERTY, NO INDUSTRY

Slide from serfdom toward free producers who keep what they make. Lords freed serfs for purely selfish reasons, because free producers work harder. The result looks like enlightened morality, but Smith credited it to the invisible hand.

WHY SERFS DON'T HURRY
A person who can acquire no property, can have no other interest but to eat as much, and to labour as little as possible.
Adam Smith, Wealth of Nations
QUESTION III

How will we share it?

Property decides who gets what, and government exists to protect property. In commercial society the produce splits three ways: rent, wages, and profit. The split between wages and profit is settled by a struggle between unequal sides.

THE THREE ORDERS

Who owns the land and the tools? Smith said that where workers own the means of production, the whole produce of labor belongs to the laborer. Once stock and land are owned by others, those owners take a share before the worker is allowed to produce.

pp. 47–48; split is illustrative.
WHAT GOVERNMENT IS FOR
Civil government, so far as it is instituted for the security of property, is in reality instituted for the defence of the rich against the poor.
Adam Smith, Wealth of Nations

TWO SMITHS

Smith of class conflict

  • Property creates government, and government defends the rich.
  • Labor alone creates wealth; profit is what owners take from it.
  • Masters overpower workers through wealth, law, and opinion.
VS

Smith of the invisible hand

  • Selfish acts add up to a harmony no one intended.
  • Vain lords and penny-turning merchants create freedom.
  • What looks like human wisdom is "the wisdom of God."

The authors argue that both streams of later economics, the one that stresses conflict and the one that stresses harmony, trace their roots to this tension in Smith (and later Ricardo). p. 44
