// MISSION BRIEFING

The laws that divide the harvest

Ricardo read The Wealth of Nations in 1799 and spent the rest of his life building a model of capitalism so tight that both Marxists and mainstream economists still claim him. His big idea: as population grows, farming spreads to worse land, rents rise, and profits get squeezed toward zero. That made the landlords the enemy of everyone else.

Friend vs. rival: Ricardo & Malthus

Close friends and lifelong opponents. Ricardo admired Malthus's population theory and took it on whole. On the great fight of the day, landlords vs. capitalists, he took the capitalists' side. See where they agreed and clashed →

// QUESTION 01

What will we make?

Food sets the limit on everything, so make it cheap: import grain instead of plowing ever-worse land. And don't make everything yourself. Each country should specialize in what it's relatively best at and trade for the rest.

// TRANSMISSION · ON FREE TRADE
This pursuit of individual advantage is admirably connected with the universal good of the whole. David Ricardo, Principles

Whose "sum of enjoyments"?

Ricardo said free trade raises the "sum of enjoyments." The authors ask: whose? If only landlords and capitalists drink the imported wine while workers lack warm clothes, the market counts that as a gain.

pp. 120–21

Reproducible vs. rare

Ricardo's theory covers goods that labor can multiply "almost without assignable limit." A few things, like rare statues, scarce books, and peculiar wines, are priced by the wealth and whims of buyers instead. Ricardo set them aside as unimportant.

pp. 98–99

Capital comes first

For Ricardo, a nation's welfare rides on accumulating capital. Shrink it, and "the resources of the people and the state will fall away with increasing rapidity, and distress and ruin will follow." What gets made should keep profits, and so investment, alive.

The authors' point: the labor theory let him focus on exactly the forces that drive accumulation, which a theory based on individual utility never could.

p. 99
// QUESTION 02

How will we make it?

By labor alone. Nature does its work for free, machines are just labor done earlier, and every extra bushel from worse land takes more labor than the last.

Time is money: capital tied up

Profit compounds for as long as a capitalist's money is tied up. So $100 of wages paid years before a product is finished adds more than $100 to its price.

The book's example ($100, 4 years, 10% → $146.41) is on pp. 107–8.

// QUESTION 03

How will we share it?

Through conflict. With output fixed, higher wages mean lower profits. And as farming spreads to worse land, landlords take a growing share at the capitalists' expense, until profits vanish and growth stops.

The rent ladder · Ricardo's own example

Plots yield 100, 90, 80… quarters of corn net. Competition among farmers equalizes profit at whatever the worst plot yields; the rest goes to the landlords as rent.

Workers' share

Ricardo agreed with Malthus that workers would always drift back toward subsistence: more income, bigger families. And wages, "like all other contracts," should be "left to the fair and free competition of the market, and should never be controlled by the interference of the legislature." His sympathy, the authors note, lay with the capitalists. See Malthus on the poor →

pp. 95, 115, 118
// FINAL ANALYSIS

Two Ricardos

The class-conflict Ricardo

  • Profits must fall when wages rise.
  • The landlord's gain is wholly someone else's loss.
  • Machines can throw workers out of work.
  • A mother country can profit by squeezing its colonies.
VS

The free-market Ricardo

  • Free trade raises everyone's "sum of enjoyments."
  • Gluts are impossible; supply creates its own demand.
  • Wages should be left to the free market.
  • Capitalism's property relations are natural and eternal.

The authors see Smith's contradiction again. The labor-theory side of Ricardo sees conflict everywhere, but conflict implies change, and Ricardo denied that capitalism would ever change. So he fell back on an invisible hand. pp. 120–23
